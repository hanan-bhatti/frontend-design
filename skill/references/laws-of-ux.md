# The 21 Laws of UX

A guiding set of principles and best practices for UX, UI, and product designers, steeped in psychology.

---

## 1. Heuristics (Mental Shortcuts)

### 1.1 Aesthetic-Usability Effect
Users tend to perceive aesthetically pleasing designs as more usable.
- **Takeaway**: Ensure designs are both flawlessly functional and aesthetically pleasing.

### 1.2 Fitts’s Law
The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.
- **Takeaway**: Make touch targets (buttons, menu items) large enough and position them in easily accessible locations.

### 1.3 Goal-Gradient Effect
The closer a user gets to completing a task, the more motivated they are to complete it.
- **Takeaway**: Provide clear progress visibility (bars, microcopy) to keep users engaged.

### 1.4 Hick’s Law
The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.
- **Takeaway**: Minimize options to reduce decision fatigue. Break complex processes into smaller steps.

### 1.5 Jakob’s Law
Users spend most of their time on other sites and prefer your site to work the same way.
- **Takeaway**: Leverage existing mental models and familiar patterns/icons.

### 1.6 Miller’s Law
The average person can only hold 7 (± 2) items in their working memory.
- **Takeaway**: Chunk information into meaningful, manageable groups to reduce cognitive load.

### 1.7 Parkinson’s Law
Any task will expand to fill the time allotted for its completion.
- **Takeaway**: Design tasks to be even quicker than expected using features like autofill and clear estimates.

## 2. Gestalt Principles (Visual Perception)

### 2.1 Law of Common Region
Elements share a common region if they are within a clearly defined boundary.
- **Takeaway**: Use borders, spacing, and color to define regions and group related elements.

### 2.2 Law of Proximity
Elements that are close to each other tend to be perceived as a single group.
- **Takeaway**: Signal relationships by placing related labels and inputs close together.

### 2.3 Law of Prägnanz
The brain interprets complex images in the simplest form possible.
- **Takeaway**: Favor simple, straightforward shapes (squares, circles) for important UI elements.

### 2.4 Law of Similarity
Elements that look similar are perceived as part of the same group.
- **Takeaway**: Maintain consistency in styling (e.g., all links should look the same) to build functional connections.

### 2.5 Law of Uniform Connectedness
Visually connected elements are perceived as more related than elements with no connection.
- **Takeaway**: Use lines, arrows, or colors to group different elements visually.

---

## 3. Cognitive Bias

### 3.1 Peak-End Rule
People judge an experience based on how they felt at its peak and at its end.
- **Takeaway**: Identify and optimize the most intense and final moments of the user journey.

### 3.2 Serial Position Effect
Users best remember the first and last items in a sequence.
- **Takeaway**: Place the most important elements at the beginning and end of menus or pages.

### 3.3 Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect)
The element that differs from the rest is the most likely to be remembered.
- **Takeaway**: Use distinct shapes or colors to help primary actions (CTAs) stand out.

### 3.4 Zeigarnik Effect
Uncompleted tasks stick in memory more than completed ones.
- **Takeaway**: Provide visual indicators of incomplete tasks to encourage return and engagement.

---

## 4. Additional Principles

### 4.1 Doherty Threshold
Productivity skyrockets when a system and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures neither has to wait.
- **Takeaway**: Provide instant feedback for every action. Use progress bars if a process takes longer.

### 4.2 Occam’s Razor
The simplest solution is usually the best.
- **Takeaway**: Remove any element that doesn't add specific value without compromising functionality.

### 4.3 Pareto Principle
80% of effects come from 20% of causes.
- **Takeaway**: Identify and focus most efforts on the features that have the biggest impact on UX.

### 4.4 Postel’s Law (Robustness Principle)
Be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others.
- **Takeaway**: Anticipate varied user inputs and design to accommodate them (e.g., accepting different date formats).

### 4.5 Tesler’s Law (Complexity Conservation)
Every system has a certain degree of complexity that cannot be reduced.
- **Takeaway**: Aim for simplicity in the user experience, but don't oversimplify to the point of breaking functionality.
